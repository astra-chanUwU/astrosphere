# Illustrations of Paradise Lost by Gustave Doré

Gustave Doré’s illustrations for John Milton’s *Paradise Lost* turn the poem’s immense scale into a sequence of theatrical encounters. Angels cross storm systems, fallen spirits gather beneath impossible skies, Satan moves through rock and fire, and Eden opens as a world of intimate bodies, animals, water, and leaves. Doré’s line is most powerful when it makes these different realms feel connected: heaven, chaos, Hell, and the garden all seem to share the same engraved surface.

This gallery follows the complete supplied sequence of distinct numbered plates, from plate 1 through plate 50. The images are presented as a visual study set rather than as a claim about the original order or captions of a particular edition. The numbering preserves the source filenames and makes it possible to move through the whole run without losing the material history of the files.

The early plates establish the violence and hierarchy of the fallen world. Crowds of angels become armies; defeat becomes a landscape; Satan’s body is repeatedly placed against distances that make even a monumental figure appear vulnerable. Doré gives the rebellious spirits a dramatic physical presence, but he also surrounds them with consequences: broken ground, engulfing cloud, machinery, serpentine forms, and the blank pressure of the void.

As the sequence moves toward Eden, the line changes temperature. The darkness does not disappear, but it becomes foliage, water, bark, and shadowed paths. Adam and Eve appear within a living world that is beautiful precisely because it is not empty. Animals, plants, rivers, and distant light make paradise feel like an environment that can be entered—and therefore left.

The final plates return the poem to loss, knowledge, and departure. Doré’s figures are often small inside the image, yet their gestures carry the emotional weight of the scene: a raised arm, a turned head, a body bent beneath an angel’s warning, or a pair moving away from the garden. The engravings make *Paradise Lost* legible as a drama of scale, but also as a drama of attention. What matters is not only where the figures stand, but what they can still see.

Together, these fifty plates show why Doré’s *Paradise Lost* has remained such a durable visual companion to Milton’s poem. The images do not simply translate descriptions into pictures. They build a world of thresholds—between rebellion and obedience, grandeur and ruin, spectacle and intimacy—where every vision seems to contain the possibility of its opposite.
