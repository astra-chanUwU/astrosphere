# The Shah’s War in Dhofar

In the early 1970s, Iran’s army fought in a war that is easy to forget when the history of the Pahlavi state is reduced to Tehran. The battlefield was Dhofar, Oman’s southern province, where Sultan Qaboos bin Said was trying to defeat a long-running insurgency with the help of Britain, Iran, Jordan, and local allies.

This was not a conventional communist invasion of Oman. It was a regionalized insurgency: a movement that began with local grievances in Dhofar and became increasingly Marxist, organized as the Popular Front for the Liberation of Oman and the Arab Gulf. Its main external support came from Marxist South Yemen, across the border. Soviet and Chinese assistance formed part of the wider Cold War environment around the rebellion.

## Qaboos asks Tehran

Qaboos took power in 1970 and inherited the Dhofar war. His government had the formal authority of a sultanate, but its armed forces were small and the conflict was being fought in difficult mountain terrain. During Qaboos’s diplomacy with Tehran, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi agreed to help.

The request mattered because it made Iran’s intervention an invitation from the Omani government rather than an unannounced occupation. Iran’s first deployments developed in 1972–73, and by late 1973 an Iranian expeditionary force was operating in Dhofar. The U.S. government later described the Iranian force as an infantry brigade with air support, sent at Oman’s request.

## Why the Shah intervened

The Shah’s public language was anti-communist, but the strategic calculation was broader. Iran and Oman faced the Strait of Hormuz together. A hostile revolutionary regime on Oman’s southern flank could threaten shipping routes, oil movement, and Iran’s position as the Gulf’s strongest military power.

Historian James F. Goode therefore treats the intervention as both “assisting our brothers” and defending Iran itself. The Shah was helping another monarch, but he was also acting on the belief that instability around Oman could become a direct security problem for Iran.

## The Iranian task force

Iranian soldiers, helicopters, transport aircraft, artillery, and logistical units gave the Sultan’s side the capacity to sustain offensive operations in the west and center of Dhofar. The combined strategy was methodical: clear an area, build fortified posts and barriers, cut the routes used by insurgents, and then move the line toward the South Yemeni border.

The Imperial Iranian Task Force did not fight alone. British officers commanded and advised much of the Sultan’s Armed Forces, Omani units supplied local knowledge and manpower, and Jordan provided military assistance. The relationship was not frictionless: the Iranian command distrusted British oversight, while British officers questioned Iranian planning and field performance. Even so, the task force made an important contribution to the Sultanate’s eventual victory.

## Victory, with a complicated legacy

By 1975, the insurgents had lost most of their territorial position. The fall of key bases and the tightening of the border campaign broke the rebellion’s ability to operate openly. Qaboos announced the end of the major war in December 1975, while final pacification and reintegration continued afterward.

The victory was not the work of Iran alone. British counter-insurgency, Omani reforms, local defections, economic development, Jordanian support, and changes in South Yemeni assistance all mattered. But the Iranian intervention supplied a decisive regional weight at the moment the Sultanate needed it.

For the Pahlavi state, Dhofar was a rare example of the Shah’s regional-security doctrine becoming a sustained combat operation. For Oman, it became part of the origin story of the modern sultanate—and one reason the relationship between Muscat and Tehran survived the 1979 Iranian Revolution.
