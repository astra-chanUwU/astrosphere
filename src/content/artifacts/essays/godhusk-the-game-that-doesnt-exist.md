I first met *Godhusk* as an image, not as a game. A rust-colored body assembled from machinery, bone, cables, and something that looked uncomfortably close to a face. It had the density of a screenshot from a game I had somehow missed: a cult release, perhaps, trapped on an obscure console and remembered only by a few people who were there.

There is no such game.

That absence is the trick, but it is not a cheap one. *Godhusk — Rebirth* is a 128-page “LORE Game Book” by Plastiboo, published by Hollow Press. It is designed like an official guide to an old videogame: creatures have names, rooms have conditions, objects have uses, and the reader is constantly warned that the wrong decision can close off a path forever. The manual is not documenting a missing game. It is manufacturing the memory of one.

![Godhusk Rebirth cover art: a biomechanical vessel stands beside the title and a small “Rebirth” prompt.](/media/art/godhusk/rebirth-cover.webp)

## The guidebook is the world

The book’s premise is wonderfully compact. “A vessel is born anew,” Hollow Press says. The bearer of a dead person’s will crosses the ruins of a derelict planet, encountering secrets, dangers, and monstrosities. The choices offered are not heroic in the usual sense: spare the innocent, drink poison, punish the wretched, free your soul, or burn the world.

The language sounds like the back of an old game box, but the images make it feel like a manual recovered from inside the game itself. The interface is not decoration around the fiction. It is part of the fiction’s reality.

![A character introduction describes worn bones, an endless tomb, incompleteness, and the hope of being carried by a vessel.](/media/art/godhusk/introduction.webp)

The opening character is already exhausted before the journey begins. The body has become a problem to be managed: bones wear down, time slips away, and the self is reduced to a burden that may need another body to carry it. This is the first major idea in *Godhusk*: the body is not a stable home. It is equipment, shelter, prison, and inventory slot at the same time.

The book calls its synthetic bodies **vessels**. A vessel is made for a function, but its original purpose may have been forgotten. It has a life system, a mind cell, and modular parts that can be replaced or modified. A head can be removed. Limbs can be exchanged. A weapon is described as indispensable, but even that sounds less like a possession than an organ with a job.

![A vessel diagram labels the head, torso, weapon, and limbs of a modular living machine.](/media/art/godhusk/vessel-living-machine.webp)

This is body horror expressed as a menu screen. The grotesque becomes frightening because it is practical. The question is not simply “what happened to this creature?” It is “what part should I remove, and what will that expose?”

## Rust Town and the fantasy of a peaceful ruin

The world is not uniformly hostile. Rust Town is a settlement of vessels that have retained enough reason and desire to want an escape from the violence outside. Its residents have stopped consuming bile, and this refusal has made them almost immobile. The town is peaceful because its inhabitants have given up one of the processes that keeps them moving.

![Rust Town is a secluded vessel settlement built from enormous rounded bodies, ladders, and suspended structures.](/media/art/godhusk/rust-town.webp)

That detail is classic *Godhusk*: every social arrangement is also a biological compromise. Safety is possible, but only by becoming still. The town’s defensive system no longer threatens its residents, yet it also makes the settlement incapable of ordinary change. Peace has not healed the world. It has paused one part of its decay.

Nearby, the Trophy Hall offers a more sinister version of preservation. The room contains rare limbs and weapons, displayed as valuable objects. A collector named Zigk wants to make beautiful flesh eternal. The player can enter by defeating him and stealing his key, or avoid the confrontation and hide.

![The Trophy Hall is a secret room where Collector Zigk displays rare limbs and weapons as trophies.](/media/art/godhusk/trophy-hall.webp)

The collector’s desire is almost sympathetic until the interface explains what it means. To preserve the body is to remove it from living use. The trophy is “eternal” precisely because it no longer belongs to anyone who might need it.

## Everything has a condition

In a conventional RPG, an item is a thing you acquire. In *Godhusk*, an item often feels like a small organism or a moral decision. Sapioma’s seed, for example, grows through five stages. It needs tears. Once it reaches its final stage, it becomes a strange living object that can be removed and stored in the inventory.

![Five stages of Sapioma’s seed show a small growth becoming a flower with a pale face.](/media/art/godhusk/sapioma-stages.webp)

The seed is not just a collectible. It asks the player to wait, gather emotion, and decide whether a living thing should remain rooted or be transformed into an item. The mechanics are disturbing because they turn care into extraction.

The same logic appears in the pages about MOPOS. These automatic flying devices attach to the hand and carry integrated life systems, blood bulbs, weapons, and sensors. Some are built for combat; others collect items or detect enemies.

![MOPOS entries classify small automatic flying devices by life system, weapon type, and projectile.](/media/art/godhusk/mopos-limb.webp)

The MOPOS page is funny in the way a very strange technical manual can be funny. It is also bleak. The hand is not merely holding the machine. The machine is attached to the hand, and the hand can extract its blood bulbs to refill its own. The boundary between player, tool, and parasite is already gone.

## A bestiary of failed purposes

The creatures of *Godhusk* are not monsters because they are irrational. They are monsters because they still have purposes.

RIPPER is a rare hostile vessel, strong and agile, originally created as a security measure. After the fall of Obek, it continues searching for traitors and trying to find an artificial god. EGGET-B is smaller: an elusive automaton, a moving container that carries valuable items and can be looted after destruction.

![RIPPER and EGGET-B appear as opposing kinds of machine: a hostile security vessel and a small valuable-bearing automaton.](/media/art/godhusk/ripper-and-egget-b.webp)

This is one of the book’s most effective reversals. A creature can be beautiful, pathetic, useful, or terrifying without changing its underlying function. The player encounters the world through the vocabulary of a guidebook—hostile, rare, lootable, restricted—but the artwork keeps insisting that every entry used to be part of a larger society.

That tension is why the world feels older than its explanations. The text tells us what a thing does. The image makes us wonder what it once was.

## Speech from beyond

The most openly mythic pages are presented as transmissions rather than ordinary conversations. A figure in a forest speaks of an abandoned “ever-brighter” presence, grieving children, a world sunk into darkness, and mercy as the only thing left.

![A masked figure in a dark forest delivers a speech from beyond about abandonment, grief, pride, and mercy.](/media/art/godhusk/speech-from-beyond.webp)

The language is deliberately grand, but the presentation keeps it from becoming simple fantasy exposition. We do not receive a clean history of the gods. We receive a damaged signal, a voice with a theology but no secure authority. The world may have been abandoned by a god, or it may only have inherited the emotional consequences of believing that it was.

The Oracle page makes that uncertainty visual. Its figure is wrapped in cables, organs, and symbols, while the screen below offers numbers, icons, equipment slots, and a sentence about a snake hidden in plain sight.

![The Oracle combines a grotesque cable-bound figure with a videogame statistics screen and cryptic prophecy.](/media/art/godhusk/oracle.webp)

The screen suggests that knowledge can be measured. The image suggests that knowledge has become another form of infection.

## The game that only exists in the reader

Some pages describe exact consequences. If the player kills SAGE-V2, an “INF unit” and an iron brick can be obtained from the corpse. Leaving the building after the killing triggers an attack. The object and the danger are connected by a chain of events that feels like a walkthrough waiting to be discovered.

![A destroyed SAGE-V2 leads to an iron brick and an INF unit called “New Beginning,” turning violence into an item route.](/media/art/godhusk/inf-unit-dialog.webp)

Other pages offer spaces that sound like secrets from a game guide. A monumental funerary figure hides a staircase. A humming sound stops when a rare limb is retrieved. The player is encouraged to imagine walking through the interior of a giant dead structure, listening for the moment when the environment responds.

![A tiny player figure crosses a funerary structure toward a humming statue and a rare replacement limb.](/media/art/godhusk/funerary-pillar.webp)

Nothing moves on these pages. There is no controller, no save file, no combat animation, no failure screen. Yet the instructions produce an experience in the reader’s mind. We mentally test routes. We imagine missing an item. We feel the irritation of discovering a secret too late.

That is the real horror of the nonexistent game: the manual has enough rules to make us imagine our own mistakes.

## Why the fake game feels real

Plastiboo’s earlier *Vermis* books use the same basic spell, but *Godhusk* shifts it from dark-fantasy dungeon crawling into a dying science-fiction ecology. Its monsters are vessels. Its settlements are bodies. Its gods are systems. Its inventory is a collection of organs, memories, weapons, and unfinished transformations.

The work succeeds because it understands that a videogame is not only its rendered images. It is also the feeling of a rule that has not yet been explained, a door that may open only if the player has done something unreasonable, and an object whose importance becomes clear several hours after it was found.

![A vessel moves through a monumental interior, making the player character appear tiny against the world’s funerary scale.](/media/art/godhusk/funerary-pillar.webp)

The guidebook format lets *Godhusk* preserve all of that without building a game. It gives us the residue of interaction: the warning, the item description, the route, the consequence, the possible ending. The absent game becomes a space for the reader’s imagination to complete.

![A wounded SAGE-V2 and the “Iron Brick” entry show how Godhusk turns a scene into a chain of possible consequences.](/media/art/godhusk/inf-unit-dialog.webp)

So the question is not whether *Godhusk* should eventually become a real videogame. A real adaptation might be wonderful, but it would also close the gap that gives the book its power. The imagined game can contain every route at once. It can be impossible, unfair, beautiful, and unfinished forever.

That may be what a lore book is really preserving: not a game, but the private feeling of having discovered one.
