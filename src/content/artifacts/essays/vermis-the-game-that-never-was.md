The first strange thing about *Vermis* is how quickly it activates a memory you do not actually have.

You see the cover and your brain supplies the rest: a dungeon crawler bought secondhand, a save file from somebody else’s memory card, a strategy guide with a missing map, a game whose opening you never understood but whose images stayed with you for years. The cover looks less like an illustration than the surviving evidence of a game that once passed through the world.

There was no such game.

![Vermis cover art shows a pale dark-fantasy figure holding a sword in front of a black field framed by red and blue.](/media/art/vermis/onyx-main-banner.webp)

*Vermis I — Lost Dungeons and Forbidden Woods* is a 128-page LORE Game Book by Plastiboo, published by Hollow Press. The publisher calls it an artbook series and a world-building exercise inspired by old dungeon crawlers. It can be treated as the official guide to a game that does not exist—because it is not a game at all.

That distinction is the whole spell. *Vermis* does not ask us to pretend that we are playing. It asks us to imagine what playing would have felt like if the only thing left was the manual.

## The manual becomes a haunted object

Classic game guides are practical objects. They tell you where to go, what to collect, which enemy is weak to fire, and what terrible decision you made three rooms ago. *Vermis* borrows that authority, then turns it against the reader.

Its pages do not explain the world cleanly. They place fragments of a world inside familiar categories: character cards, creature descriptions, routes, sanctuaries, towers, crypts, and warnings. The format promises that the information can be used. The atmosphere tells us that using it may only make the journey stranger.

![Three Cloisters of the Towers cards describe devotees who have abandoned humanity, sought enlightenment, or been consumed by transformation.](/media/art/vermis/cloisters-of-the-towers.webp)

The Cloisters of the Towers are a good example. The cards look like a bestiary or class guide, but the three figures describe different forms of spiritual ruin. One has lost humanity and become dangerous. One remains in a conscious lethargy, repeating mantras. Another is slowly consumed while trying to become an illuminated being.

The card format makes these people legible as types. The writing makes them feel like failed individuals. *Vermis* is always doing both things at once: turning a person into a category, then leaving just enough detail for the category to hurt.

## A world built from impossible thresholds

The Eclipse Towers are monuments raised in search of enlightenment. They are sealed, but devotees still dwell inside them. Mantras can be heard through the walls. Their architecture rises above the ground and sinks beneath it, as if the towers were not built so much as grown through the world.

![The Eclipse Towers rise and sink through a pale landscape, their sealed interiors still carrying the mantras of devotees.](/media/art/vermis/eclipse-towers.webp)

This is the geography of *Vermis*: every location is also an argument about perception. A tower is a religious monument, a dungeon, a vertical map, and a body with something alive inside it. A crypt is a route through history. A forest is not simply a place where monsters live; it is an environment that seems to remember what the player did there.

The Solar Crypt is presented as a path belonging to Gurvek the Great. A strange landscape sits beside diagrams, symbols, a face in the dark, and the repeated warning that bones cannot be picked from this place. The scene is both a location entry and a refusal.

![The Solar Crypt page combines a purple landscape, a route title, strange symbols, and a dark figure beneath a warning about bones.](/media/art/vermis/solar-crypt.webp)

The instruction is almost comically specific. You cannot pick bones from this place. It tells us that the game has rules, but it also makes the rule feel mythological. Perhaps the bones are protected. Perhaps they are too dangerous. Perhaps the game simply refuses to let the player perform the action that would make the image ordinary.

## The monster is a situation

In a normal bestiary, a monster is a problem with statistics. In *Vermis*, a monster is more often a situation the player has entered too late to understand.

The Sage-Faced Beast lies across the page like a discovery at the edge of a ruined structure. Something small emerges from within, a mixture of blood and brimstone, while the Wayfarer approaches cautiously. The text does not give us a clean combat encounter. It gives us the feeling of discovering a huge body that may already have noticed us.

![The Sage-Faced Beast lies before a cloaked Wayfarer while a small strange entity emerges from within the structure.](/media/art/vermis/sage-faced-beast.webp)

Another page stages a fight with a monster as an exchange of possible rewards. The Wayfarer reaches for a sword; the creature lunges. Beneath the scene are two items—Monstrous Bones and a Shiny Amulet—with their chances of being obtained.

![A Wayfarer faces a lunging monster while the page lists the possible rewards of Monstrous Bones and a Shiny Amulet.](/media/art/vermis/fighting-monster-items.webp)

This is where the fake game becomes especially convincing. The item table is not needed to make the illustration frightening, but it changes the reader’s posture. We start calculating. Is the fight worth it? Which item matters? Does the chance of obtaining it justify the risk?

The manual has made us players without giving us anything to control.

## The tragedy of the Last Stroke

The Last Stroke is one of the most quietly devastating images in the set. A headless giant stands completely still, more like a macabre statue than a living enemy. A knight, exhausted after failing to stand, drops her longsword with a loud clang and collapses too.

![A headless giant stands motionless while an exhausted knight drops her sword in the aftermath of the Last Stroke.](/media/art/vermis/last-stroke.webp)

The page refuses the usual rhythm of fantasy combat. There is no triumphant blow, no clear victor, no reward screen. The fight ends in mutual exhaustion. The giant is not defeated so much as left standing. The knight is not killed so much as unable to continue.

That stillness is one of Plastiboo’s signatures. The images feel like action scenes paused at the exact moment when action stops explaining anything. The player is left with aftermath, not resolution.

## The Glass Purgatory

The phrase “Only those who observe the world with eyes of glass will find their way into the Purgatory” sounds like a clue, a prophecy, and an instruction for an input puzzle. The accompanying image shows a figure between tall structures, using a beam of vision or light to reveal a route.

![A small figure enters the Glass Purgatory between tall structures, guided by a beam of light and directional marks.](/media/art/vermis/entering-glass-purgatory.webp)

The reader can imagine what the game would require: equip an object, stand in the correct place, look through the right surface, or simply understand the sentence in a way that the game never teaches you. The image is diagrammatic, but not helpful. It shows the solution while keeping the problem mysterious.

The Guardians of the Glass sharpen that mystery. The Conjoined Witch carries the Scepter of the Primordial Glass, while the Conjoined Warrior carries its sword. Both are made from accumulated bodies. The witch contains collective intellect; the warrior is an amalgam of skilled and powerful dead fighters.

![The Guardians of the Glass are presented as two cards: the Conjoined Witch and the Conjoined Warrior, protectors of the Primordial Glass.](/media/art/vermis/guardians-of-the-glass.webp)

They are not simply bosses. They are tools made into beings, or beings made into tools. Their identities are collective, but their roles are singular. They protect the heart of the Purgatory because the Purgatory has made them for that purpose.

## Mist, mirrors, and the problem of the self

The second book, *Vermis II — Mist & Mirrors*, expands the same fictional-game grammar. Hollow Press describes it as the second book in the series, another act of world-building inspired by old dungeon crawlers. Its small-print promise is beautifully cruel: “Thy flesh doth not belong to thee.”

![The Vermis Mist & Mirrors cover shows four distorted faces floating above the title.](/media/art/vermis/mist-and-mirrors.webp)

The title announces a world where perception is unstable. The faces on the cover look like masks, corpses, reflections, or character portraits corrupted by too much looking. *Vermis II* does not merely ask who the player is. It asks whether a face can still belong to a person after it has become an image in a manual.

![Vermis II — Mist & Mirrors presents a dark forest and a green animal-like figure beneath an official-guide frame.](/media/art/vermis/vermis-ii-mist-and-mirrors.webp)

The second cover is more overtly game-like: a framed scene, a title treatment, interface symbols, and a green figure emerging from darkness. It feels like a sequel because the visual language has become more confident. It also feels more dangerous because the book now knows exactly how much information it can withhold.

## The gods are not safe to worship

The Worshipers of Oggol page shows an armored figure facing a one-eyed presence in the dark. The scene is devotional, but nothing about it feels protected. The god—or whatever is being worshiped—appears as an opening in the world with a face attached.

![An armored worshiper raises a sword toward the one-eyed figure known as Oggol.](/media/art/vermis/worshipers-of-oggol.webp)

The Bone Hunters are more direct. They serve a dark god in different ways: robbing victims, sacrificing their own bodies, committing massacres, or searching sand-covered tombs for bones to pile on altars. Their shared goal does not make them a community. It gives their violence a common direction.

![The Bone Hunters stand together as a group of masked figures devoted to different forms of violence, sacrifice, and tomb-robbing.](/media/art/vermis/bone-hunters.webp)

This is another place where *Vermis* feels less like a fantasy setting and more like a collection of religious nightmares. The question is not “which god is good?” It is “what does devotion make a body willing to do?”

## The Goblin Princess and the possibility of kindness

The Goblin Princess interrupts the cruelty. Her story describes a gentle flower growing amid blood, horror, and depravity. She develops genuine devotion toward the creatures around her, awakens feelings of kindness and love, and eventually leads a rebellion against her mother.

![The Goblin Princess is shown as a pale, luminous figure beside a story of kindness, rebellion, and the rise of the Green Sun Kingdom.](/media/art/vermis/goblin-princess.webp)

The rebellion is violent—the Queen is thrown into the maw of the beast of her own blood—but the story still offers something close to hope. The new kingdom promises that not a single drop of goblin blood will ever be spilled again.

In another work, this might be the beginning of a heroic quest. In *Vermis*, it feels like a fragile legend that survived because somebody wrote it down. The kindness matters precisely because the surrounding world has trained us not to expect it.

## Why the nonexistent game works

*Vermis* exists in the gap between a world and its documentation. The books give us lore, but they also give us the sensation of play: the locked route, the obscure condition, the item with a suspiciously specific use, the enemy who might be worth fighting, and the location where the manual says you cannot pick up the bones.

Academic writing on the series has described this as a fictional game existing both alongside a secondary world and inside a factual book. That is exactly the strange double life of *Vermis*. The game is fictional, but the book is real. The world is invented, but the guide has weight in your hands. The player is absent, yet the reader keeps making decisions.

![A lone figure faces the enormous silence of a dark fantasy landscape, framed like a page from a lost manual.](/media/art/vermis/entering-glass-purgatory.webp)

That is why *Vermis* does not feel like a concept for a game. It feels like the remains of one. Its incompleteness is not a production problem waiting to be solved. It is the atmosphere.

If the game were made, the manuals would become supplementary. The maps would be clarified. The monsters would receive hit points. The impossible places would become levels with entrances and exits. The reader would stop inventing the missing rules because the rules would finally be there.

For now, *Vermis* is better as a haunting: a dark-fantasy game guide from a world that never shipped, where every page gives you just enough information to imagine the room beyond it.
