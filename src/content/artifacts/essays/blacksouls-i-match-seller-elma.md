# Match Seller Elma

Match Seller Elma is the BLACKSOULS companion who sells matches, hides forbidden books, and turns curiosity into a route through grief. Her story borrows from *The Little Match Girl* but refuses to leave the tale at its familiar ending: Elma’s visions become encounters with family, Goose, the Pied Piper, and the authorial machinery behind Elysium.

> **Adults-only warning:** Elma’s route includes death, sexual violence, rape branches, nudity, demonbeast fights, and disturbing family material. The H-scene section identifies the guide’s documented scenes without reproducing explicit prose.

## Appearances in BLACKSOULS I

### Going to Elma

Elma’s match shop is easy to overlook, which suits a character who survives by making herself useful and small. Her shop dialogue establishes her as shy, observant, and more literate than the setting expects. The forbidden books hidden near her stall hint that her imagination is not a harmless escape.

## Miscellaneous route branches

### Demonbeast Elma

The demonbeast encounter is the route’s clearest image of corrupted innocence. Elma remains recognizable even when the body and battle system insist that she has become an enemy.

### Playing in the Water and Elma’s father

The water sequence and her father’s arrival expose the difference between Elma’s private fantasy and the family structures that produced her fear. Her route repeatedly asks whether a child can author a future when every adult around her has already written the ending.

## Personality

Elma is shy, imaginative, and hungry for a gentler world. She is not simply innocent: the forbidden literature and her knowledge of stories give her a sharp inner life. What looks like passivity is often a calculation about how to survive adults who have already decided what she is.

## Design

Elma’s match-seller silhouette is warm and fragile at once. Her shop clothes, pale face, and expressive eyes make the match motif readable before the route explains it; later images let that same design carry fear, anger, and fairy-tale absurdity.
