# The Adversary!

The figure in Sir Joseph Noel Paton’s *The Adversary!* does not leap out of the dark. He waits inside it. Small, vertical, and almost swallowed by a red-black enclosure, he faces us with the concentrated stillness of someone who has already seen what we have not.

Paton gives the Devil no theatrical battlefield and no crowd of the damned. There is only the figure, a suggestion of wings or surrounding darkness, and the long staff or weapon gathered between his hands. The image is frightening because it withholds the event. We are not shown the attack, the temptation, or the punishment. We are shown the adversary waiting for the moment when one of those things might begin.

The painting’s narrow format intensifies that pressure. It feels less like a window onto a large world than a vertical opening cut into a wall. The figure fills it without quite occupying it. His horns and shoulders rise into the upper darkness, while his legs descend toward a ground that is barely distinguishable from the surrounding brown and crimson. Everything seems close, but nothing is fully available to the eye.

Paton’s Satan is an embodied idea. The title announces an opponent, but the body makes that opposition psychological. The face is not distorted into a cartoon of wickedness. It is direct, severe, and watchful. The eyes seem to hold the viewer in place. His hands close around the central object, giving the pose a ritual concentration: he is not merely standing in hell; he is keeping guard over it.

The red around him is important. It is not the bright, expansive red of flames. It is a buried red, like heat seen through ash or blood under skin. The colour presses toward the figure from the edges, making the darkness feel active. The background does not simply hide details; it seems to breathe around him. The adversary is less a person placed in a cave than the point at which the cave has become conscious.

Paton belonged to a Victorian culture fascinated by the borderlands between religious painting, romantic medievalism, fairy imagery, and moral allegory. The National Galleries of Scotland describe him as an artist of detailed compositions drawn from biblical episodes and imaginative stories based on romantic myths and legends. That mixture helps explain the painting’s peculiar atmosphere. *The Adversary!* is scriptural in its title and moral charge, but its visual language also belongs to the dream, the fairy tale, and the illustrated nightmare.

The work’s longer subtitle invokes the biblical image of punishment that does not end: a worm that does not die and a fire that cannot be quenched. Paton does not paint that sentence literally. There are no writhing masses and no broad inferno. Instead, he paints the consciousness of punishment—the presence that watches over the place where punishment is imagined. The image is not a map of hell but a portrait of its sentinel.

That makes the figure strangely close to a portrait, even though he is an allegorical being. Paton gives him a face with a particular intensity, a posture with a particular economy, and hands that seem to know exactly what they are holding. The demon is not a force of random chaos. He is organized, patient, and self-possessed. His danger comes from deliberation.

Look at the lower half of the painting, where the red and black forms become least certain. The feet do not establish a stable floor. The body appears to stand, but the picture never quite proves that standing is possible. This uncertainty gives the figure a suspended quality: he may be waiting in a cavern, in a dream, or in the instant before the image itself resolves.

The most unsettling thing about *The Adversary!* is therefore not what Paton shows, but what he makes the viewer supply. We imagine the victim outside the frame. We imagine the command, the temptation, the fire, the sound that has not yet happened. The adversary’s power is partly the power of a blank around him. He guards an unseen story, and our own imagination enters the darkness to finish it.
