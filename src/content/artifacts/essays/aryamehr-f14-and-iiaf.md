# Aryamehr’s Tomcats

There is a particular kind of historical amnesia surrounding the Imperial Iranian Air Force. People remember that Iran bought American aircraft under Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. They often forget what that purchase represented: a sovereign Iranian strategy, an industrial-scale modernization program, and the emergence of Iran as the strongest military power in the Persian Gulf.

The F-14 Tomcat was the clearest symbol of that achievement.

## The decision to buy the Tomcat

In the early 1970s, Iran faced a serious air-defense problem. Soviet reconnaissance aircraft, including the high-altitude MiG-25, could approach Iranian airspace at speeds and altitudes that made older interceptors inadequate. Iraq was also expanding its Soviet-equipped air force.

Aryamehr wanted an aircraft that could protect Iran’s enormous territory, its oil infrastructure, and the approaches to the Persian Gulf. The F-14 was not a cheap multirole fighter. It was a sophisticated air-superiority interceptor built around the AWG-9 radar and the long-range AIM-54 Phoenix missile.

The Shah chose capability over fashion. Iran ordered 80 F-14A Tomcats in 1974, along with Phoenix missiles. That decision made Iran the only country besides the United States to operate the Tomcat.

The choice was not simply a matter of purchasing a prestigious aircraft. It was a declaration that Iran would not accept a subordinate security role in its own region.

## Did the Shah save Grumman?

The modern company Northrop Grumman did not yet exist—the Northrop-Grumman merger came in 1994. The aircraft was made by Grumman Aerospace Corporation, which was facing a serious financial crisis while the F-14 program itself was under pressure.

The popular story says that the Shah saved Grumman from bankruptcy. The precise version is even more interesting. A 1974 U.S. diplomatic telegram records that Grumman had a cash-flow problem and that the U.S. Navy and Iran agreed to share financial support for the F-14 program. Iran’s 80-aircraft purchase brought the United States nearly $1.8 billion, excluding missiles.

Contemporary Congressional reporting also described an Iranian bank loan to Grumman of approximately $75 million. Grumman’s financial trouble was serious enough to threaten the production of the Tomcat. The Shah’s purchase therefore did not merely add Iranian aircraft to an American production line; it helped keep that line alive at a crucial moment.

So “the Shah saved Grumman” is patriotic shorthand, but it is not an invented story. Iran’s order and financing were central to the survival of the F-14 program, while the U.S. Navy and American institutions also participated in the rescue.

## The IIAF was more than its aircraft

The Imperial Iranian Air Force became formidable because it combined modern equipment with training, organization, and technical depth.

By 1971, a U.S. assessment judged the Iranian Air Force clearly superior to Iraq’s in motivation, training, organization, and technical maintenance. Iran already operated F-4 Phantom IIs and F-5 fighters, and its transport fleet included C-130 Hercules aircraft. The F-14 order then added a long-range interception capability that no other regional air force possessed.

Iranian aircrew trained in the United States and Iran. Maintenance personnel trained on engines, avionics, radar, and weapons systems. A new F-14 complex was built at Khatami Air Base, and the IIAF developed the institutional infrastructure needed to support a highly advanced aircraft rather than treating it as a foreign trophy.

By 1979, around 120 Iranian pilots and radar-intercept officers had been trained for the Tomcat, with more still in the pipeline. The IIAF’s strength rested on people as much as machines: professional volunteers, demanding standards, and a culture that treated technical competence as part of national power.

## Iran’s regional position

The Pahlavi military buildup took place within a coherent strategic picture. Iran controlled the northern side of the Gulf and stood near the Soviet Union, Iraq, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Indian Ocean. The Shah understood that Iran’s geography made weakness dangerous.

By the mid-1970s, Iran had become the dominant military power in the Gulf and the world’s largest arms importer. The air force was expanding toward a force of hundreds of combat aircraft, while the army was acquiring modern armor, helicopters, air defense, and artillery. The Iranian Navy was also regarded as the strongest navy in the Persian Gulf.

This was not simply an American military presence painted with Iranian roundels. American technology mattered, but the strategic decision was Iranian. Tehran selected the systems, paid for them, built the bases, trained the personnel, and assumed responsibility for defending Iran’s own interests.

## Aryamehr’s legacy in the sky

The Shah’s critics often describe the F-14 purchase as extravagant. A Pahlavi reading sees something else: a ruler using Iran’s oil wealth to buy time, deterrence, technical knowledge, and strategic independence.

Aryamehr’s Tomcats gave Iran a capability that its neighbors could not match. They protected the country’s airspace, complicated Soviet reconnaissance, discouraged Iraqi adventurism, and made Iran indispensable to the American security architecture in the Gulf—all while rescuing an aircraft program that might otherwise have collapsed.

The Islamic Republic inherited those aircraft, bases, and trained personnel after 1979. Even after the revolution severed the political relationship with the United States, Iranian crews kept the Tomcats flying and used them in the Iran–Iraq War. That endurance was possible because the Pahlavi state had built more than an inventory. It had built an air force.

The F-14 was therefore not merely an American fighter bought by Iran. It was one of the clearest monuments to the Shah’s vision of Iran as a modern, sovereign, and regionally decisive power.
