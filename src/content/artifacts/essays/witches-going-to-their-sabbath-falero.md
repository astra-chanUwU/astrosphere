# Witches Going to Their Sabbath

Luis Ricardo Falero’s *Witches Going to Their Sabbath* is a painting of movement that never quite becomes a journey. The witches fly, crouch, tumble, and cling to one another through a storm-coloured sky, but the picture offers no clear destination. There is no distant mountain or moonlit clearing toward which the procession travels. Instead, the whole canvas seems caught inside one enormous current, a whirlpool of bodies, hair, wings, limbs, animals, and smoke.

That lack of stable direction is the first thing that makes the painting feel so alive. Falero fills the surface with figures, yet the eye cannot settle on a single narrative centre. A woman rides a goat near the lower middle. An older witch grips one of its horns and leans into a younger red-haired figure. Nearby, a dark wizard or demon pushes into the group. Above them, other bodies drift across the clouds: some seem to fly under their own power, some ride animals, and some appear to be falling. The Sabbath is less a destination than a temporary condition of weightlessness.

Falero painted the work in 1878, in a period when occult subjects offered European artists a way to combine the fashionable, the forbidden, and the spectacular. His witches are not presented as solemn figures from folklore. They are theatrical bodies, arranged for the pleasure and unease of looking. Their nudity makes the scene sensual, but the sensuality is continually interrupted by awkward poses, grinning faces, animal forms, and the crowded pressure of the composition. Beauty and grotesquerie are not opposites here. They are two surfaces of the same fantasy.

The upper portion of the painting makes that tension especially clear. A long, pale figure stretches horizontally across the clouds, arms and legs extended as if the body itself were a flying instrument. To the left, a bat opens its wings beside a cluster of witches whose poses are more comical than graceful. Farther right, a skeletal bird hangs in the air like a bad omen that has wandered into a theatrical procession. The sky is not empty space behind the figures; it is a material they disturb, a grey-blue atmosphere smeared with their passage.

At the centre, Falero uses the goat as both vehicle and anchor. Its body gives the scene a point of animal heaviness, a reminder that flight in this world still depends on something that can be grasped, ridden, or dragged through the air. The goat’s horns become handles. The witches hold on to the creature and to one another, forming a chain that is at once playful and precarious. Their magic does not free them from physical dependence. Even supernatural movement needs a body beneath it.

The painting’s lower figures are more grounded and more disturbing. A red-haired witch turns her face upward while a dark, sharp-featured figure reaches past her. In front of them, another woman bends around the goat, her pose almost acrobatic. At the bottom edge, more bodies are cut off by the frame, as if the procession continues below the visible world. Falero makes the canvas feel too small for the sabbath it contains. Figures enter and leave at every edge, and the image seems to continue beyond its own borders.

On the right, the mood changes without becoming less theatrical. A pale skeletal creature floats among the clouds, while below it a corpse-like figure and a salamander-like animal introduce a more deliberate language of death and alchemy. These details are not arranged as a single emblematic code. They accumulate. The skeleton, the bat, the goat, the fire-spirit, and the naked bodies all belong to an occult vocabulary, but Falero lets that vocabulary remain excessive and unstable. The viewer is invited to recognize symbols without being given a final interpretation that would tame them.

There is also a strange comedy in the painting. The witches do not always look powerful. Some seem to be losing their balance; others are caught in poses that resemble acrobatics or dance. The red-haired figure looks startled rather than triumphant. The older witch clings to the group with the practical desperation of a passenger on a vehicle that has begun to move too fast. Falero’s imagination is erotic, but it is not solemn. He understands that the supernatural can be seductive precisely because it is a little ridiculous.

This is where the painting differs from a conventional scene of demonic terror. Fear is present, but it keeps dissolving into spectacle. The skeletal forms threaten the living figures, yet the living figures are just as visually commanding. The women’s bodies occupy the lightest areas of the sky; the monsters emerge around them as companions, obstacles, and props. Darkness does not erase desire. Desire becomes one of the forces that keeps the darkness in motion.

The title promises a ritual, but the painting withholds the ritual itself. We see the witches going, not arriving. Their destination remains outside the frame, and that absence matters. The Sabbath is imagined as an event that can only be approached through perpetual movement. The group may be flying toward a ceremony, a devil, a mountain, or an invented horizon, but the painting’s real subject is the passage between ordinary life and forbidden fantasy—the moment when bodies leave the ground and the rules of the world become negotiable.

Falero’s composition finally makes looking feel like a form of flight. The eye moves from the bat to the pale figure above, down through the goat and the red-haired witch, across the skeletal creatures, and back into the storm. Each route reveals another collision of elegance and deformity. Nothing stays still long enough to become a single idea. *Witches Going to Their Sabbath* is beautiful because it refuses to settle into beauty, and unsettling because its monsters are carried along by the same exhilarating freedom as its witches. The whole painting is an airborne threshold: a world suspended between desire, laughter, and the dark thing waiting beyond the clouds.
