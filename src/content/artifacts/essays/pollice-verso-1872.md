# Pollice Verso

Jean-Léon Gérôme’s *Pollice Verso* (1872) turns a Roman arena into a theatre of looking. A wounded gladiator lies in the dust while the victor waits for the crowd’s decision. Above them, thousands of spectators lean over the stone railings, turning one death into a shared public event.

Gérôme fills the scene with convincing surfaces: polished armour, patterned carpets, marble, banners, skin, dust, and the hard geometry of the arena. Yet the painting’s real centre is not the fallen body or the raised weapon. It is the small gesture repeated across the seats—the turned thumbs that seem to travel through the crowd and arrive at the gladiator below.

The title means “with a turned thumb,” but the precise Roman gesture is not as certain as the familiar modern image suggests. That uncertainty makes the painting even more interesting. Look at who participates, who hesitates, and who is reduced to spectacle. Is the arena controlled by the emperor, the crowd, the ritual, or the image itself? Let the answer remain in motion as you look.
