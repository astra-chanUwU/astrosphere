# My Dream IIAF for 2026

This is a fantasy article. It is not a real procurement plan, a prediction, or an attempt to pretend that the Imperial Iranian Air Force survived unchanged into the twenty-first century.

It is simply my dream: if Aryamehr’s Iran had continued, modernized, and entered 2026 on its own terms, what would I want the Imperial Iranian Air Force to look like?

My answer begins with one principle: Iran should not look like everybody else.

I do not want a generic regional fleet assembled from whatever aircraft happen to be in fashion. I do not want the standard package of F-15s, F-16s, and F-35s that makes every wealthy air force look like a different-colored version of the same American catalogue.

I want a distinctly Iranian force—Northrop-heavy, Boeing-supported, technically ambitious, and built around aircraft that carry a sense of identity.

## The Northrop core

The backbone would be the Northrop F-20 Tigershark.

The F-20 was the fighter that never got its chance. It took the compact, economical spirit of the F-5E and turned it into something much more capable: a single powerful engine, modern radar, better acceleration, modern weapons, and a far higher ceiling of performance.

In my alternate timeline, Iran becomes one of the F-20’s first major customers in the 1980s. The IIAF buys the aircraft in large numbers, supports Northrop’s production line, and turns the Tigershark into the affordable mass fighter of the Persian air arm.

The F-20 would not replace the F-14. It would complement it. The Tomcat would be the elite long-range interceptor; the Tigershark would be the aircraft that could actually fill squadrons, generate sorties, defend air bases, and be maintained in depth across Iran’s enormous geography.

Then comes the beautiful impossible centerpiece: the Northrop YF-23.

In this fantasy, Aryamehr’s long relationship with Northrop does not end with the F-5 and F-20. When the YF-23 loses the American Advanced Tactical Fighter competition, Iran funds the design’s survival. Northrop develops a Persian version: the YF-23P Black Shahin.

The Black Shahin is Iran’s stealth air-superiority aircraft—not a copy of somebody else’s F-35, but a long-range, high-speed, low-observable fighter shaped around Iran’s distances and strategic geography. It is the aircraft that makes the IIAF unmistakably itself.

## Persian Tomcats

The F-14 fleet remains the crown jewels.

In 2026, I want newer Persian Cats: a rebuilt and expanded F-14D-derived fleet with modern engines, digital cockpits, AESA radar, infrared search and track, modern electronic warfare, secure datalinks, and a new family of long-range air-to-air missiles.

The Tomcat would retain its original Iranian purpose—defending a huge country against high-speed threats and controlling the air approaches to the Gulf—but it would no longer be frozen in the 1970s.

The IIAF would keep the F-14 because it is not merely an aircraft. It is a national symbol: the machine Aryamehr chose when Iran wanted the most capable interceptor available.

## The Shahin 2000 Phantom

The F-4E fleet would receive an Israeli-style modernization, inspired by the Kurnass 2000 and later Phantom upgrade programs.

I would call the Iranian version the Shahin 2000.

The Shahin would not pretend to be a stealth fighter. Its job would be different: heavy strike, maritime attack, anti-radar work, reconnaissance, and long-range precision weapons. It would receive a modern radar, digital mission computer, improved navigation, electronic warfare, structural refurbishment, new cockpit displays, and upgraded engines.

The Phantom is too large, too useful, and too historically important to discard casually. A modernized F-4 could remain the IIAF’s heavy old lion: expensive to operate, but still valuable when range and payload matter more than visual elegance.

## Boeing support and the air bridge

Aryamehr’s Iran also invested heavily in Boeing aircraft, so the support fleet should be unmistakably Boeing-centered.

The dream IIAF would operate new tanker aircraft, not just fighters. A modern air force without tankers is a short-legged air force, and Iran is too large to accept that limitation.

The tanker force would include a mix of Boeing 747- and 767-derived tankers, supported by a smaller number of long-range strategic transports. The point is endurance: keeping Tomcats, Tigersharks, Phantoms, and Black Shahins in the air over the Gulf, the Caspian, the Zagros, and the Makran coast.

## Reconnaissance above the clouds

The U-2 Dragon Lady would be a special situation asset.

It would not be an everyday patrol aircraft, and it would not be sent casually into an environment saturated with modern surface-to-air missiles. It would belong to a highly controlled strategic reconnaissance command, used when Iran needed information that satellites, drones, and conventional aircraft could not provide.

The U-2 is also exactly the kind of strange, elite machine that suits this fantasy: difficult, rare, politically sensitive, and almost mythological.

## An Iranian helicopter tradition

The helicopter force would preserve the best of the Shah-era inventory while modernizing it aggressively.

The AH-1J Cobras would become Cobra 2000s with modern sensors, weapons, engines, protection, and night-fighting systems. Iran already operated Cobras, so this is a continuation of an existing Iranian strength rather than a completely new direction.

The UH-1 Huey would remain in utility, medical evacuation, liaison, and training roles. Not every aircraft needs to be glamorous. The Huey survives because it is useful.

The CH-47 Chinook fleet would expand under Boeing support. Iran’s mountains, deserts, islands, and long borders demand heavy lift. Chinooks would move troops, artillery, supplies, rescue teams, and engineering equipment where roads are slow or nonexistent.

And then there is the AH-56 Cheyenne.

Historically, the Cheyenne was cancelled. In my fantasy, Iran becomes the customer that saves it. Lockheed develops a Cheyenne II for Iranian Army Aviation: a fast, heavily armed, armored attack helicopter with advanced sensors and a distinctive high-speed role between a conventional helicopter gunship and a low-flying attack aircraft.

It is wonderfully impractical—and that is precisely why it belongs in the dream fleet.

## The force I want

My imaginary 2026 IIAF would therefore look something like this:

- F-20 Tigershark: the mass light fighter and F-5 successor.
- F-14 Persian Cat: the long-range national interceptor.
- F-4E Shahin 2000: heavy strike, maritime attack, and reconnaissance.
- YF-23P Black Shahin: stealth air superiority and deep penetration.
- Boeing tanker and transport fleet: the range and endurance layer.
- U-2 Dragon Lady: exceptional strategic reconnaissance.
- AH-1J Cobra 2000: modernized attack helicopter.
- UH-1 Huey: utility, rescue, and training.
- Boeing CH-47 Chinook: heavy lift.
- AH-56 Cheyenne II: the resurrected high-speed gunship.

This fleet would be expensive, eccentric, and difficult to sustain. It would also be unmistakably Iranian.

That is the whole point. The dream is not to imitate the Arab Gulf air forces or assemble a standard NATO catalogue. The dream is an IIAF with its own silhouette in the sky: Northrop’s daring, Boeing’s reach, Israel’s modernization expertise, Lockheed’s strange cancelled machines, and Aryamehr’s belief that Iran should operate at the highest technical level available.

Not realistic. Not a real plan. Just my ideal Persian air force.
