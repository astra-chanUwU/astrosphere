# The Mossadegh Myth

One of the biggest modern historical distortions repeated is the idea that the late Shah of Iran was installed by a coup in 1953, or that Iran had a democratic government which was suddenly removed by foreign hands.

This is a Pahlavi-oriented reading of that story: one concerned with constitutional succession, the crisis around oil, and the Iranian actors who are too often removed from the picture.

![Portrait of Mohammad Reza Pahlavi](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-portrait.jpeg)

![Portrait of Mohammad Mossadegh](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-portrait.jpeg)

## The Shah was already king

In 1953, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi had already been king for twelve years. He became Shah through constitutional succession, following his father, Reza Shah the Great. He had been Crown Prince before 1941. This was not a foreign installation.

![Mohammad Reza Pahlavi with Reza Shah](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-and-reza-shah.jpeg)

## Razmara and Mossadegh's appointment

Before Mossadegh, Iran already had a sitting prime minister: Haji Ali Razmara. In 1951, Razmara was assassinated by the same radical Islamist current that had tried to assassinate the Shah in 1949.

![Portrait of Haji Ali Razmara](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/haji-ali-razmara-portrait.jpeg)

![Mohammad Reza Pahlavi and Haji Ali Razmara](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-and-razmara.jpeg)

The Shah and parliament were under violent political pressure to appoint Mossadegh. He was appointed through parliamentary procedure, and the Shah formally approved him as Prime Minister under the Iranian constitution.

![Mohammad Reza Pahlavi addressing parliament](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-parliament.jpeg)

![Mossadegh as Prime Minister](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-prime-minister.jpeg)

What did Mossadegh do after becoming Prime Minister? He pardoned the Islamist assassin who killed his predecessor. Let that sink in.

![A Fada'iyan-e Islam figure](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/fadaiyan-e-islam-figure.jpeg)

## The democratic image

Mossadegh is called a champion of democracy in the West today. But in 1952, when a parliamentary election was underway and his side was losing, he cancelled the election.

![Mossadegh with supporters](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-with-supporters.jpeg)

Mossadegh was never elected by popular vote as prime minister, and cancelled a popular vote because he was losing. There was no public election where Mossadegh defeated another candidate for the premiership. That fact is conveniently left out by academics who repeat the same distortion against the Shah.

![Mossadegh smoking](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-smoking.jpeg)

![Mossadegh with his wife](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-and-wife.jpeg)

## Oil and the economic crisis

Modern historians often say Mossadegh was great because he nationalized Iran's oil. In this reading, he did not build a functioning nationalization; he cancelled a contract with the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company and called it nationalisation.

The result was a sudden collapse in Iranian oil sales—by some accounts, around 90 percent—and a serious economic crisis.

![Oil nationalisation demonstration](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/oil-nationalisation-demonstration.jpeg)

![Oil workers at work](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/oil-workers.jpeg)

The industry had been operated by Europeans who were also training Iranians. They were suddenly expelled; Iran could not simply continue production at the same scale.

## The Qajar connection and emergency power

Another detail often left out: Mossadegh was a member of the Qajar royal family, the dynasty that preceded the Pahlavis. Reza Shah replaced the Qajars through constitutional procedure and parliamentary support. Mossadegh represented the old regime that had left Iran weak and destitute.

![Qajar dynasty portrait](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/qajar-dynasty.jpeg)

![Mossadegh in Qajar dress](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-qajar-portrait.jpeg)

The Shah had plans for a more sustainable nationalisation of Iranian oil. By abruptly cancelling oil contracts, Mossadegh created crisis and turmoil, then used that chaos to claim what he himself called dictatorial powers.

![Mossadegh in office](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-in-office.jpeg)

Mossadegh also attempted to abolish the Senate, a constitutional power assigned solely to the Shah. From this perspective, it was an unconstitutional power grab.

![Mossadegh leaving by airplane](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-airplane.jpeg)

## The Farman

Seeing this power struggle, Mohammad Reza Pahlavi used his constitutional authority to dismiss Mossadegh through a *farman*: a royal decree written into the constitution.

![Mohammad Reza Pahlavi reading a farman](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-farman.jpeg)

The Shah had the constitutional power to dismiss the Prime Minister, and did so in the interest of Iran's economy and its people.

![Mohammad Reza Pahlavi and Farah Pahlavi before a public crowd](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mohammad-reza-pahlavi-and-farah.jpeg)

## Operation Ajax and Iranian agency

“Operation Ajax proves there was a coup” is often treated as the whole story. But the CIA narrative was useful to the agency at the time: it presented one of its early covert operations as a success, helped establish its credibility, and justified its methods.

![Mossadegh and a CIA emblem](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-and-cia.jpeg)

Kermit Roosevelt had CIA and British support, but the operation itself was not the entire event. Even the United States and Britain asked Roosevelt to halt and leave when their effort appeared ineffective.

![Kermit Roosevelt portraits](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/kermit-roosevelt.jpeg)

As one of the CIA's early covert operations, making Ajax appear decisive was crucial to the agency's self-image.

![CIA personnel](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/cia-personnel.jpeg)

The Iranian army under General Zahedi ultimately carried out Mossadegh's arrest, not the CIA alone. Tehran saw significant violence as Iranian demonstrators and military units clashed with Mossadegh's men.

![General Zahedi](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/general-zahedi.jpeg)

![Iranian army tanks in Tehran](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/iranian-army-tanks.jpeg)

An Iranian armored column and anti-Mossadegh civilians surrounded Mossadegh's residence. Crowds chanted “Zendebad Shah!”—Long live the Shah—as Mossadegh was removed from power.

![Mossadegh under arrest](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-arrest.jpeg)

![People gathered on an army tank](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/people-on-tank.jpeg)

## A story still repeated

Today, the democracy story is often promoted in Western academia and by people who want to frame Iran only through Western imperialism. From this perspective, Mossadegh was never a widely popular figure inside Iran, and regular Iranians did not hold him in the place later narratives assign him.

![Later portrait of Mossadegh](/media/history/iran/mossadegh-myth/mossadegh-later-portrait.jpeg)

The point is not that foreign powers had no interests in Iran. They did. The point is that the 1953 story becomes false when Iranian constitutional authority, Iranian institutions, Iranian soldiers, and Iranian crowds are erased from it.
