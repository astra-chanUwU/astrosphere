# The Wild Hunt

Franz von Stuck’s *The Wild Hunt* (1899) moves through the night like a memory of violence. A dark rider leads a procession of pale horses, broken bodies, and half-seen forms. The Wild Hunt is an old northern European myth: a supernatural pursuit crossing the sky or landscape, often linked with the dead, storms, war, and the boundary between the human world and the unknown.

The rider’s face is what makes the painting strangely difficult to leave in the nineteenth century. Seen now, his flat hair, small moustache, and fixed stare can resemble Adolf Hitler. That resemblance has produced a conspiracy theory: that the work somehow anticipated Hitler, or that Hitler modelled himself on it. But the dates do not support that story. The Musée d’Orsay dates this painting to 1899; Hitler was born in 1889, and there is no evidence that Stuck intended to paint him. The image is a mythic figure first, not a hidden portrait.

Still, the association is intriguing because images change when history catches up with them. The rider’s authority, theatrical posture, and procession of the dead can now echo twentieth-century political imagery, even though that was not the painting’s original subject. Look at the face, then look away from it: toward the horses, the fog, the red-black movement, and the figures dissolving behind him. What belongs to the old myth—and what are we bringing with us?
